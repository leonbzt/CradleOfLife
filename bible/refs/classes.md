## Generalist

**Archetype:** Mesopredator — a body plan not yet committed to any one ecological role; competent across feeding strategies, niche-opportunistic, and capable of switching.

**Claims:**
- Cambrian mesopredators occupied generalist roles before the radiation of specialist body plans. *No specific claim is asserted in mechanics — Generalist is intentionally the uncommitted launch pad.*

## Predator

**Archetype:** Active raptorial hunter. Commits to high-power ambush and pursuit; specialises toward the reef-edge niche where prey diversity is highest and the power premium pays off.

**Claims:**

### Raptorial appendage strike mechanics (`raptorial_claw`, `strike_momentum`)
Radiodont anomalocaridids — including *Anomalocaris canadensis* and *Hurdia victoria* — bore large, segmented frontal appendages with recurved spines used to capture and manipulate prey. Appendage morphology is consistent with raptorial predation on soft-bodied and lightly-armoured prey.

- Whittington, H.B. & Briggs, D.E.G. 1985. The largest Cambrian animal, *Anomalocaris*, Burgess Shale, British Columbia. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 309: 569–609.
- Daley, A.C., Budd, G.E., Caron, J.-B., Edgecombe, G.D. & Collins, D. 2009. The Burgess Shale anomalocaridid *Hurdia* and its significance for early euarthropod evolution. *Science* 323: 1597–1600.

### Penetration as the Predator entry requirement
Armoured prey were already present in the Cambrian (biomineralized SSFs, trilobite sclerites). A predator committing to the raptorial niche must demonstrate the penetration capability that justifies the power investment.

- Vermeij, G.J. 1977. The Mesozoic marine revolution: evidence from snails, predators, and grazers. *Paleobiology* 3: 245–258.

### Power × 1.25, metabolism × 0.9 — stat signature
Dedicated cursorial/raptorial predators carry a metabolic cost: high muscle mass and active pursuit increase aerobic demand but specialised tissue allocation reduces gut cross-section. The tradeoff is real and well-documented in extant analogues.

- Alexander, R.M. 2003. *Principles of Animal Locomotion*. Princeton University Press. Chapter 7 (locomotor costs vs. muscle force).

## Armored Grazer

**Archetype:** Biomineralised substrate grazer. Invests in calcified scleritome defense; works the shallow benthos where biofilm and algal mats are dense and predation pressure selects for passive armor over speed.

**Claims:**

### Fused dorsal plating (`fused_dorsal_plating`, `mineralized_spine`)
*Wiwaxia corrugata* bore a scleritome of mineralized sclerites forming a dorsal coat. Whether these were primarily defensive or served a locomotory function is debated, but the calcite investment is consistent with passive anti-predator armor in a benthic grazer.

- Butterfield, N.J. 1990. A reassessment of the enigmatic Burgess Shale fossil *Wiwaxia corrugata* (Matthew) and its relationship to the polychaete *Canadia spinosa* Walcott. *Paleobiology* 16: 287–303.
- Conway Morris, S. 1985. The Middle Cambrian metazoan *Wiwaxia corrugata* (Matthew) from the Burgess Shale and Ogygopsis Shale, British Columbia, Canada. *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B* 307: 507–582.

### Biomineralization as ecological strategy
Cambrian Problematica and early Lophotrochozoa demonstrate that biomineralization (calcite, phosphate, aragonite) co-evolved with predation pressure as a passive defense strategy — not merely as structural support.

- Knoll, A.H. 2003. Biomineralization and evolutionary history. *Reviews in Mineralogy and Geochemistry* 54: 329–356.

### Resilience × 1.25, metabolism × 0.9 — stat signature
Heavy mineralised exoskeleton increases drag and metabolic cost per unit movement; the trade is lower metabolic throughput for higher damage absorption. Documented in extant heavily-armoured invertebrates.

- Vermeij, G.J. 1993. *A Natural History of Shells*. Princeton University Press. Chapter 4 (cost of shell architecture).

## Filter Feeder

**Archetype:** Suspension feeder. Exploits pelagic plankton blooms via comb-based or ciliary filtration; high metabolic throughput at low power investment; home niche is open water where particulate food is densest.

**Claims:**

### Tamisiocaris-grade frontal filtering fan (`ciliary_fan`, `laminar_current`)
*Tamisiocaris borealis* (Lower Cambrian, Sirius Passet) bore long, comb-like frontal appendages interpreted as a suspension-feeding apparatus for capturing mesoplankton from the water column. This is the first known large-bodied active suspension feeder in the Cambrian record.

- Vinther, J., Stein, M., Longrich, N.R. & Harper, D.A.T. 2014. A suspension-feeding anomalocarid from the Early Cambrian. *Nature* 507: 496–499. DOI: 10.1038/nature13010.

### Filter-feeding as a high-metabolism, low-power strategy
Suspension feeders exploit diffuse, high-volume food sources via continuous throughput rather than pursuit. Metabolic investment is in gill/fan area and pumping mechanism; active-capture musculature is reduced. The metabolism-up / power-down signature reflects this investment pattern.

- Riisgård, H.U. & Larsen, P.S. 2010. Particle capture mechanisms in suspension-feeding invertebrates. *Marine Ecology Progress Series* 418: 255–293.

### Sustain as Filter Feeder entry requirement
Committing to the suspension-feeding niche requires demonstrating already-functional respiratory or filtration uptime — the lineage must have invested in sustained throughput before the full apparatus pays off.

### Metabolism × 1.25, power × 0.9 — stat signature
Suspension feeders show reduced predatory appendages and hypertrophied filtering/metabolic apparatus across taxa. The tradeoff is consistent and convergent.

- Butterfield, N.J. 2011. Animals and the invention of the Phanerozoic Earth system. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 26: 81–87.
